Our solar system at a glance

The solar system is made up of the Sun, which is a star, eight planets, their moons, and countless smaller bodies such as dwarf planets, asteroids and comets. The Sun holds nearly all of the system's mass, and its gravity keeps everything in orbit. Sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach Earth.

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and lies about 384,400 kilometres away on average. It is responsible for the main ocean tides and shows different phases as it orbits Earth.

Stargazing for beginners

You do not need any equipment to begin. A clear night, a dark spot and a little patience reveal the Moon, bright planets and many stars.

Simple steps

  1. Pick a dark place away from street lights, such as a rooftop, field or hill, and check that the sky will be clear.
  2. Let your eyes adjust for 20 to 30 minutes. Avoid looking at bright phone screens, or switch to a red light.
  3. Start with the Moon. Its craters are easy to see even with binoculars.
  4. Find bright planets. Planets look like steady, bright dots that do not twinkle as much as stars. Free sky-map apps help you identify them.
  5. Learn a few constellations such as Orion or the Big Dipper (Saptarishi), then use them to find other stars.
Tip: new Moon nights are best for faint objects like the Milky Way, while a full Moon is bright and washes out fainter stars.
Safety warning: never look at the Sun directly, and never with binoculars or a telescope unless they have a proper certified solar filter. Doing so can cause permanent eye damage. For solar eclipses, use eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard.

Binoculars and telescopes: what to choose

Many beginners buy a telescope too early. Binoculars are cheaper, easier to use and a great first step.

Binoculars

Models such as 7x50 or 10x50 show lunar craters, bright star clusters and Jupiter's moons. They are portable and simple.

Reflector telescope

Uses a mirror to gather light and gives good views for the price. A simple Dobsonian mount is easy for beginners.

Refractor telescope

Uses lenses and needs little maintenance, making it popular for the Moon and planets, though larger sizes cost more.

What matters most

  • Aperture, the size of the main lens or mirror, decides how much light you collect and matters more than magnification.
  • A stable mount keeps the view steady. A wobbly mount ruins even a good telescope.
  • Avoid toy telescopes that advertise extreme magnification, since they usually give poor, blurry views.
  • Try before you buy by visiting a local astronomy club or a public star party.

Common space myths, explained

  • "Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun." Seasons come from the tilt of Earth's axis, not from distance.
  • "The Moon has a permanently dark side." The Moon has a far side that we never see from Earth, yet it receives sunlight just like the near side.
  • "You can hear explosions in space." Sound needs a medium such as air to travel, and space is almost a vacuum.
  • "Stars twinkle because they flash." Twinkling is caused by Earth's atmosphere bending starlight.
  • "Astronomy needs expensive equipment." Your eyes, a sky map and a dark sky are enough to begin.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best time of year to stargaze?

Clear, dry nights are best, and the season depends on where you live. Winter and post-monsoon skies are often clearer in many regions, but check your local weather forecast.

Can I see planets without a telescope?

Yes. Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn can often be seen with the naked eye when they are above the horizon. A sky-map app shows where to look.

Is astronomy a good subject for children?

Absolutely. Stargazing, moon-phase diaries and simple models encourage curiosity and science skills. Always supervise outdoor night activity and never allow children to look at the Sun.
